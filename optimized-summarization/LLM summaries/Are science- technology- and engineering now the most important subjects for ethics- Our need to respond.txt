Executive Summary
This paper asserts that science, technology, and engineering (STE) have become the most important subjects for ethical study, arguing that the dramatic expansion of human power brought about by these fields necessitates a comprehensive reevaluation of human action. The increased capacity for large-scale consequences, illustrated by examples such as human activity potentially triggering a massive mud-volcano in Indonesia or altering the Earth's atmospheric composition to cause more powerful storms and raise sea levels, demands that ethics transform its focus. Because technology is the direct cause of this enhanced human power, those involved in its generation have a special responsibility to ensure that technology is controllable and used for good, a responsibility consistent with the goal of professional engineering societies to hold paramount the "health, safety, and welfare" of the public.

The paper argues for a dual mandate: ethics needs to study STE, and conversely, scientists, technologists, and engineers need to study ethics with great diligence, embodying ethical behavior in their professional lives. The trajectory of change mirrors past shifts; for example, when medical ethicists face novel challenges like "brain death," *in vitro* fertilization, or organ transplantation, it is often because medical technology has produced a new situation requiring moral navigation. To address this gap, the paper proposes two critical steps: first, making ethics courses mandatory for all students in STE programs, and second, significantly increasing the professionalization of STE practitioners.

This increased professionalization is crucial because too often, STE professionals function merely as parts in a machine, obediently carrying out decisions made by business managers without deeply considering the contexts and large-scale effects of their actions. If the STE professions currently lack the necessary cohesion to stop bad management decisions due to the power imbalance, professionalization offers the necessary leverage—similar to that employed by labor unions—to effectively respond. Accepting this moral charge is the only ethically acceptable solution, ensuring that the resulting technologies can be controlled and used for societal benefit, thereby determining which path humanity chooses in the face of risks that can be analyzed using the equation: risk equals harm times probability.
