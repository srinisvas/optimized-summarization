**Executive Summary**

This paper addresses the critical gap between detailed discussion of ethical concerns in AI development and the practical competencies required for translating established ethical principles into actionable design practices. The research utilizes the concept of Translational Ethical Competency (TEC), which supports a "situation-sensitive ethical approach" to AI, to investigate how ethical principles are incorporated into real-world AI system development. The methodology involved conducting a pilot study using semistructured text-based elicitation interviews with AI professionals. Initial interviews utilized four case studies—covering domains like autonomous vehicles and educational fact checking—to guide the discussion and explore how an AI designer might develop a tool while adhering to introduced ethical principles, such as nonmaleficence or autonomy. The overall approach adopted is deductive, focused on understanding the translation *process* rather than discerning specific ethics principles emerging from everyday design stories.

Reflections on the pilot study highlighted significant methodological challenges. Observations showed that interviewees often lacked confidence discussing cases where they were unfamiliar with the problem domain, particularly the third and fourth cases, which potentially obscured a full understanding of the translation details. Furthermore, interviewees required time to become familiar with the provided ethical principles framework before they could effectively apply them to the design task. For instance, one interviewee noted that the justice and beneficence principles felt unclear in a particular context. Consequently, the research protocol was revised: the number of case studies was reduced to a single scenario (the autonomous vehicle case study) while participants were asked to share two of their own AI design projects, focusing on processes they were most familiar with. The revised protocol also mandates sharing the single case study with interviewees in advance, allowing them sufficient time to reflect on the design task and apply the ethical principles framework before the interview.

The primary aim of this work is to identify best practices for incorporating ethical principles into the AI design process. Based on the updated protocol, future work involves conducting interviews with 15–20 industry engineers. The anticipated findings will enhance the understanding of how to translate AI ethics principles into actual AI design, offering practical insights crucial for AI ethics education and professional development, especially considering reports that nearly half of schools currently do not offer any computing-related ethics courses. This endeavor contributes directly to the society-wide effort needed to integrate ethics effectively across government, the private sector, and academia.
