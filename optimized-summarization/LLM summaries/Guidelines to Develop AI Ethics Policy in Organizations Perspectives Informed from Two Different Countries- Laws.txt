**Executive Summary**

This paper examines actual attempts to develop and implement ethical frameworks by analyzing the AI regulatory approaches of the United Arab Emirates (UAE) and New Zealand (NZ). The study aimed to analyze the moral concerns raised by AI along five moral dimensions grounded in these two national strategies, with the ultimate goal of developing informed recommendations for organizations crafting their own AI ethics policies. The analysis utilized the lens of five moral dimensions proposed by Laudon and Laudon, finding that while both countries have demonstrated a level of maturity in establishing such regulations, substantial work remains to be done in this domain.

The provided recommendations address a comprehensive range of key ethical considerations related to AI adoption, including data protection and ownership, accountability and responsibility, error management, physical safety, societal harms, and economic implications. The rapid expansion of AI applications—such as autonomous vehicles and AI-driven solutions in legal practices—brings forward complex questions regarding liability, such as who is responsible if an AI-based system provides an incorrect diagnosis or if a self-driving vehicle causes an accident. Furthermore, issues like massive manipulation through falsifying media and expanding threats through hacking and theft underscore that cybersecurity is becoming increasingly important, necessitating a clear understanding of whether AI can be regulated as items subject to copyright or property, or as a legal entity.

To ensure ethical accountability, the research highlights that algorithms must be developed transparently to determine the ethical responsibility of those working in AI. Predictive tools used by government must support meaningful explanations, requiring complex systems to be augmented with an "explanation system" to generate understandable explanations. Organizational guidelines suggest introducing penalties, ranging from fines to dismissal, for not adhering to codes of ethics. Informed by the current policy implementations in New Zealand and the UAE, the paper presented specific recommendations for companies and organizations to develop robust ethics policies, concluding that future work will involve investigating how the recent EU Artificial Intelligence Act will further shape AI ethics policy.
