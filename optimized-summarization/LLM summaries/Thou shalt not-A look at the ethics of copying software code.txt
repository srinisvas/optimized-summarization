Executive Summary

This paper investigates the moral acceptability and ethical compliance of software engineers regarding the copying of computer software code, addressing inherent ambiguities that persist despite codified ethical standards established since the 1970s. While historical rules mandate honoring property rights and avoiding the appropriation of intellectual output—leading to bright line rules against practices like copying software without paying for a license—real-world practice often presents "grey areas" where engineers must determine the morally correct action. This difficulty is aggravated by the industry recognition of the value associated with computer software code, driven by new developments in programming technologies and programmer expertise. For the purpose of this analysis, the research specifically focused on copyright, patent, and trade secret intellectual property rights as they relate to the copying of computer software code.

The methodology employed involved distributing a survey designed using Microsoft Excel 2007® to active members of the IEEE Region 4 via an email blast, utilizing a semantic differential scale ranging from one to seven for scoring responses. Although research findings were limited due to the sample size, the survey results provided specific insights into professional conduct. For instance, regarding confidential information gained in professional work, approximately 95% of respondents indicated they were somewhat likely, likely, or highly likely to make others aware of such work practices. Furthermore, when software engineers were asked if they were accurate in stating the characteristics of the software on which they work and avoiding misleading claims, the mean score for the fourth survey question was high at 6.5 (with a 0.8 standard deviation), and the fifth question similarly yielded a mean of 6.8 (with a 0.4 standard deviation), though two possible interpretations were identified for the polarization observed in the data.

Underlying the ethical dilemmas faced by software engineers, who possess professional ethical duties, are complex intellectual property distinctions and competing ideologies which make it difficult to determine the extent to which copying is permissible. Specifically, copyright law protects the expression of an idea but does not extend to the ideas themselves, nor does it cover "program logic, algorithms, systems, methods, concepts, or layouts." Affirmative protection for these technical aspects is instead accomplished through patent law, while maintaining trade secret status requires a person to make reasonable efforts to ensure the secrecy of the information. Future research utilizing the same software code of ethics is recommended; however, the instrument design could be modified with the use of an alternative software package to promote ease of use and better flexibility, potentially addressing issues where certain language may have confused respondents.
