**Executive Summary**

This research investigates the complex dynamics of privacy management for young digital citizens within Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, utilizing the Privacy Calculus Model (PCM) as its theoretical framework. The study employed a mixed-methods approach involving 482 participants, including 176 young digital citizens (ages 16–19), 146 parents and educators, and 160 AI specialists/developers, to analyze critical dimensions such as data ownership and control (DOC), perceived risks and benefits (PRB), trust, transparency, and education and awareness (EA). The analysis was guided by the PCM constructs to determine how perceived risks associated with sharing personal data influence perceptions of DOC in youth data-disclosure decisions, underscoring the necessity of ensuring robust student privacy while addressing varied stakeholder views.

The results highlight the complex trade-offs involved in privacy management, often constrained by varying levels of digital literacy and opaque data processes. Parental data sharing (PDS) scores were low across all groups surveyed (youth scoring 2.52; AI professionals 2.36; parents/educators 2.94), and analysis confirmed that PRB did not significantly mediate the relationship between EA and PDS. AI researchers noted that accessible data controls are often lacking, making it difficult for users to decline data collection without sacrificing useful features. Furthermore, opaque AI models increase privacy worries and decrease the willingness of young users to share information, confirming the significance of transparency and dependability in cultivating user confidence.

The study’s main contribution emphasizes that effective privacy governance requires promoting digital literacy, establishing trust through transparent and ethical design, and implementing collaborative strategies among stakeholders. EA is identified as a crucial element for enabling people to make informed choices, supporting calls for mandatory educational programs prior to AI usage, as many adolescents use AI without considering their privacy. Addressing challenges such as excessive parental intervention and low digital literacy, particularly among marginalized communities, requires focused interventions, including adaptive consent mechanisms, customized educational activities, explainable AI tools that clarify data use, and inclusive policymaking that supports robust privacy protections.
