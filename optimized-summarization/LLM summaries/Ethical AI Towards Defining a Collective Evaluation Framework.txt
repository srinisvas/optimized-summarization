Executive Summary
Artificial Intelligence is rapidly transforming high-stakes sectors, including healthcare and finance, but its integration introduces urgent ethical concerns stemming from systemic bias, opaque decision-making, and unfair treatment in domains where sensitive decisions are made. Issues like biased algorithms in hiring or lending processes underscore the necessity for transparent and accountable AI systems that can effectively monitor outcomes and ensure equitable treatment. This paper addresses these risks by proposing a novel, unified system for ethical assessment, designed to align AI outcomes with emerging global regulations, including the specific requirements of the EU AI Act. The core of this proposed framework is the concept of modular, expert-designed **ontological blocks**, which are discrete, interpretable units that encode ethical principles such as fairness, accountability, and data ownership. The foundational structure for these blocks is simple, pairing a primary concept with a binary ethical qualifier, ensuring the resulting assessment tools are both philosophically robust and operationally viable across various AI application areas.

The methodology integrates these ontological blocks, which draw from Semantic Web standards to provide scalable representations of principles like non-maleficence and equity, with the **FAIR principles** (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). This combination enhances the discoverability, usability, and scalability of the ethical framework, supporting the goal of building transparent and accountable AI systems. Demonstrated through a real-world use case in AI-powered investor profiling, the system utilizes NLP and probabilistic modeling to estimate a behavioral risk profile, enabling dynamic, context-aware, and ethically grounded risk assessment in real-time. This approach confirms that the combined structure effectively identifies ethical risks and supports compliance mandates, laying essential groundwork for responsible AI development across diverse sectors.

While the evaluation confirms that the integration of ontological blocks and FAIR principles offers a robust foundation for ethical AI monitoring, several challenges remain. Key strengths include the system’s independence from the AI’s training data and the resulting structured, modular, and interpretable ethical encoding. However, limitations involve the substantial manual effort required to construct the initial ontological blocks and a reliance on probabilistic reasoning when dealing with incomplete data, requiring human oversight if cross-references within the system prove weak. The work concludes that future efforts should concentrate on automating block creation and improving data integration to fully overcome these dependencies and realize the framework's potential for fully explainable and auditable ethical AI assessment.
