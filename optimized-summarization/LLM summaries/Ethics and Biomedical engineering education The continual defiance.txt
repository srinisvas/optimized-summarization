Executive Summary

Biomedical engineering (BME) programs face a continual challenge in defining the extent and necessary content of curriculum to ensure students attain an understanding of professional and ethical responsibility, despite the expectation that BME engineers be introduced to ethics early in their careers. Addressing this gap, this paper outlines an Ethics program of study designed to prepare professionals to perform their duties under strong moral standards, recognizing that the rapid advancements in the field of medicine and the strong multidisciplinary integration exemplified by BME have given birth to new ethical problems. The proposed curriculum encompasses a detailed integration of the fundamentals of Ethics, alongside research, professional, and social Ethics, thereby covering both the theoretical foundations and the practical application of ethical conduct in engineering.

A core component of this program involves essential topics that represent the minimal contents necessary for BME students to grasp, including an understanding of the critical role of ethical review boards within institutions and the procedures required to prepare strict research protocols for cases involving human subjects and animals. The proposed methodology suggests adopting a learning cycle, in accordance with the principles addressed by Bransford et al., which utilizes discussion settings where students research and revise published information to bring potential solutions forward. Furthermore, classes are divided into small groups representing various advocacy positions—such as government, manufacturers, regulatory agencies, and the public—for response to complex challenges with ethical implications, recognizing that some solving strategies for moral problems could be common to other disciplines like math or physics.

The critical necessity for robust Ethics education is evidenced by historical failures in the field, which highlight the severe consequences of human error and deficient quality control. For example, the period of 1985-1987 saw six patients lose their lives due to human error involving low energy radiation treatments, and a separate case involved pediatric respirators where an incorrectly placed relief valve risked applying dangerously high pressure to an infant’s lung. Similarly, the Björk-Shiley Convexo-Concave (BSCC) mechanical heart valve, which saw 86,000 valves implanted between 1979 and 1986, is known for a history of failure; although the manufacturer modified welding protocols, there was no recall of valves, and a set of guidelines was only recently approved by the US District Court for Ohio. While the individual nature of each case often makes it difficult to identify the most important information and relevant moral considerations, students must be aware that once the ethical problem is identified, a solution is possible.
