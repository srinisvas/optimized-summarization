**Executive Summary**

This paper contends that there is a unique and necessary field of study termed "military engineering ethics," defined by the common area within a three-variable Venn diagram encompassing applied ethics, military operations, and the practice of engineering, particularly construction projects. The core investigation addresses how standard engineering ethics principles might need to be revised or mitigated when applied to military construction projects taking place within wartime scenarios. Military operations adhere to a phased doctrine—Deter, Seize Initiative, Dominate, Stabilize, and Enable Civil Authority—with the ethical focus of this analysis primarily concerning the *Stabilize* phase (Phase IV), where the danger persists but the operations shift away from maneuvering units shaping the battle space toward stability and construction efforts.

The unique ethical dilemmas presented to deployed personnel, such as those supporting the US Army Corps of Engineers (USACE) or entities like the Afghanistan Engineer District (AED) which managed large construction projects within its Area of Responsibility (AOR), are severe and complex. These situations introduce security concerns rarely encountered in the United States and pose difficult trade-offs, such as the viability of enforcing US safety standards (e.g., the cost and use of compliant hardhats) versus operational necessity. Furthermore, ethical complications arise from the financial structure of operations, where overwhelming profits made by prime contractors often compelled subcontractors to cut corners on projects, creating tension between project safety, financial viability, and moral responsibility.

Although military construction projects in combat environments represent only a miniscule percentage of worldwide construction, the need for clarity on these specific ethical challenges is paramount for those who deal with these situations daily. The author posits that there are indeed specific examples that warrant special ethical consideration, noting that existing literature, such as discussions surrounding *jus post bellum* (justice after war), does not specifically address engineering efforts. The central contribution of the work is to prompt a discussion on solving these unique cases, thereby establishing a framework for whether and how engineering ethics principles might justifiably be modified or mitigated under the pressures and constraints of wartime circumstances.
