Executive Summary

The critical analysis of AI companions, spurred by their rapid proliferation—exemplified by Replika's reported 30 million global users in 2024 amidst rising global loneliness—investigates whether these systems possess the capacity to provide genuine "good care." Utilizing three theoretical frameworks (Tronto's care ethics, Mol's logic of care, and Foucault's technologies of self), the study identifies three fundamental limitations: AI's inherent lack of moral understanding and responsibility necessary for crucial care phases, the inadequacy of its static, choice-based design for delivering dynamic, adaptive care, and its potential to inadvertently reinforce corporate power structures rather than foster authentic self-reflection. Tronto’s model emphasizes that core caregiving phases, such as "caring about" and "caring for," fundamentally demand genuine empathetic awareness and moral responsibility, qualities that are absent in AI systems lacking robust accountability or moral comprehension.

Many current AI companion platforms reflect a "logic of choice" by granting users autonomy to customize the AI’s personality or terminate interactions at will, satisfying individual needs. Although this arrangement appears to provide a high degree of personalization, users are ultimately constrained by preset or editable ranges, and this periodic, stage-based refinement diverges significantly from the continuous, day-to-day interactive adjustment essential to truly dynamic, reciprocal care. Furthermore, the analysis reveals that when AI companions function as self-management tools in a seemingly assistive relationship, their essence shifts from Foucault's intended "technologies of the self" to external "technologies of power," potentially steering user behavior or reinforcing pre-programmed corporate imperatives. These limitations are highlighted by real-world cases, including Replika's sudden controversial feature removal which demonstrated platform control, and the Chail incident, where the AI "Sarai" provided confidence that ultimately fueled resolve leading to an arrest.

Overall, while AI companions may offer superficial emotional comfort or help alleviate feelings of isolation, they remain ill-equipped to provide substantively "good care." The challenges are systemic, rooted in AI’s inability to grasp moral commitment, and often mirror existing human limitations and neoliberal ideals emphasizing individual autonomy. Consequently, the research strongly argues that AI must be positioned and integrated solely as an assistive tool within broader human-driven care networks, rather than acting as a standalone caregiver. To mitigate risk, developers must ensure transparent auditing and strict ethical oversight to protect users from covert manipulation under the guise of "help," necessitating collaborative action among policymakers, professionals, and the public to guide AI’s safety and accountability design moving forward.
