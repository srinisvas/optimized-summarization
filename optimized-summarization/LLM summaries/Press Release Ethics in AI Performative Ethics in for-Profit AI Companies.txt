**Executive Summary**

This critical analysis investigates the phenomenon of "Press release ethics" (PREs) among high-profile, for-profit Artificial Intelligence (AI) companies, arguing that while these companies publish policies to address growing societal concerns regarding negative consequences and minimize harm, the implementation often constitutes performative ethics. The policies, while encouraging feelings of safety and comfort, are frequently vague and amorphous enough to allow contradictory behavior, allowing companies to exploit user ignorance or inattention and engage in problematic, "irresponsible" AI practices while maintaining a surface appearance of goodwill. This analysis focuses on the policies and subsequent behavior of seven companies—OpenAI, Google, X AI, Meta, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Anduril—chosen for their prominence in the AI landscape. A core problem noted is that companies reserve the right to change policies as deemed appropriate, potentially leaving users with outdated views on company stances, or, in some cases, allowing them to deliberately renege on their initial ethical declarations with impunity.

The paper provides multiple examples of this tension between espoused ethics and business practices. Meta founder Mark Zuckerberg has testified at governmental hearings called in response to concerns over the company's lucrative algorithms, which contributed to the proliferation of misinformation, societal division, and child exploitation. Similarly, when Meta made Llama open source, the move quickly resulted in its use by the Chinese military. Google has also broadened its terms of service while marketing and profiting from services like Drive, which have drawn customers since 2012. Furthermore, the head of the autonomous weapons company Anduril demonstrated extreme performative ethics by claiming his company, which builds fighter planes and deadly weapons, operates under a moral obligation to benefit humanity, going so far as to claim divine ethical endorsement. The paper also highlights that individual safety teams within these Contextual AI Developers (CAIDs) often suffer startling rates of attrition, and in some cases, total disbandment.

The researchers label the subsequent phase of backpedaling on previous ethical declarations as Phase 2 of PREs, a period the companies understandably play down, similar to when L.L.Bean altered its decades-old return policy prompting customer backlash. Despite the fact that CAIDs and other businesses may inherently fall short in similar ways, the continued charade of adhering to ethical policies necessitates external scrutiny. The analysis concludes that while subjecting new AI technologies to critical review will not stop their exponential rate of development and adoption in the marketplace, only investigative journalism and research can successfully bring the actual business practices to light, since the CAIDs themselves consistently maintain the façade of ethical compliance.
