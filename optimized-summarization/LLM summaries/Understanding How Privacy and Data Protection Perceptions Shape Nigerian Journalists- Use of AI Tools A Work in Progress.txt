Executive Summary

This paper investigates the understanding and perceptions of privacy, security, and data protection among Nigerian journalists incorporating Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) tools into their professional workflows. Addressing a recognized gap in scholarly research concerning AI adoption and associated privacy concerns in the African context, the study focused on Nigeria due to its status as Africa’s largest democracy and its existing AI policy framework that acknowledges and seeks to promote data privacy and security, underpinned by GDPR-like legislation guaranteeing citizens' rights to data protection. The research aimed to articulate the mental models journalists hold regarding the privacy policies and data handling practices implemented by the AI companies responsible for the tools they use, particularly given that journalists frequently handle sensitive national and local issues and possess data sets vulnerable to exploitation.

The study employed a qualitative approach involving interviews with 20 Nigerian journalists, drawn from small independent newsrooms up to global corporations, all of whom were mandated to have used Artificial Intelligence tools professionally within the six months preceding the interview. Through reflexive thematic analysis, findings indicated that Nigerian journalists generally possess a less-than-ideal mental model of the data handling and privacy policies employed by AI companies, which introduces potential risk into AI-assisted journalism. Despite this deficit in understanding, participants noted a willingness to share data with AI companies when they perceive it offers valuable exchange, such as improved technology or journalism-specific agentic AI. This perception of risk is further complicated by evidence showing that even sophisticated models like LLMs lack internal reasoning on what sensitive information to share or otherwise, making reliance on AI governance challenging.

The work concludes that while journalists are fundamentally willing to "do the right thing" concerning data protection, they are currently hampered by a lack of time, resources, and requisite guidance to effectively manage these risks. Having answered the research question on how perceptions influence AI tool usage, the authors recommend that addressing the challenges affecting how Nigerian journalists use AI requires substantive collaborations between the journalists and the AI companies themselves. Ultimately, the paper underscores the critical need for further AI-in-journalism research in lesser-studied regions, recognizing that while journalists' mental models may be inadequate, their resulting professional decisions are inextricably rooted in those immediate perceptions.
