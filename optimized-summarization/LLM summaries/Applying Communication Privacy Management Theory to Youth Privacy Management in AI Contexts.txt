Executive Summary
The rapid integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies into the lives of young digital citizens has escalated privacy concerns, prompting this study to apply Communication Privacy Management (CPM) Theory to understand how youth and critical stakeholders navigate these challenges. The methodology employed a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative data from structured questionnaires with qualitative insights from open-ended responses collected from 306 participants, comprising 146 AI professionals, 127 parents and educators, and 33 youths aged 16-19. The research utilized five validated constructs—Data Ownership and Control (DOC), Parental Data Sharing (PDS), Perceived Risks and Benefits (PRB), Transparency and Trust (TT), and Education and Awareness (EA)—to frame the analysis. Data processing was conducted using a Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) approach via smartPLS software, which employed a path-weighing structural model scheme and generated 5,000 subsamples for analysis at a significance level of 0.1, subsequent to receiving ethical approval (reference number #103116).

Descriptive statistics revealed distinct perspectives among the demographics regarding data ownership, education, transparency, and trust. The PLS-SEM model identified key influences on youth privacy management, confirming that Education and Awareness (EA) significantly influences DOC and PRB, underscoring the critical importance of privacy education in facilitating effective boundary management strategies. Similarly, Transparency and Trust (TT) positively influences DOC, reinforcing the role of transparency in building trust and reducing boundary turbulence. However, the complexity of privacy behaviors was highlighted by the finding that Parental Data Sharing (PDS) exhibits a significant negative impact on DOC (β = -0.170; p < 0.01). The qualitative analysis further emphasized a shared sense of helplessness among all stakeholders due to a lack of understanding and concerns about data misuse; for instance, one young participant remarked, "I do not know enough about them to be concerned about what I should be concerned about." Although included in the constructs, PRB exhibited a low score, suggesting poor reliability and questioning its appropriateness for further analysis.

This study underscores the importance of integrating diverse stakeholders' perspectives in AI system development, emphasizing the need for collaborative policymaking, implementing user-centric design practices, and enhancing privacy education to empower young digital citizens. By promoting practical tools and using real-life examples, such educational programs can help individuals develop a more nuanced understanding about how to balance convenience and privacy risks when using AI systems, ultimately empowering young individuals to establish boundaries and exercise autonomy over their data. While the research advances the application of CPM Theory to AI contexts, future studies should expand demographic diversity, refine constructs for improved reliability, and employ random sampling methods and longitudinal designs to validate these results and further inform privacy-centric AI system designs.
