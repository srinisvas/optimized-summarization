**Executive Summary**

This paper introduces an ethical framework for the phenomenon of speech processing, grounding its methodology in Speech Act Theory from the Philosophy of Language. The framework systematically maps ethical considerations across the distinct stages a spoken item navigates—input, processing, and output—by employing the core concepts of illocutionary force and illocutionary content. The illocutionary force, which encompasses elements such as sincerity and strength, is characterized as inherently belonging to the speaker and often becomes an inseparable part of the collected speech data, even in simple voice recording contexts. This distinction is critical, as privacy-related ethical issues can be explained by considering specific features (the 2nd, 6th, and 7th) of the illocutionary force, reinforcing the understanding that the ethical problems relating to speech data possess a distinct nature.

By integrating the illocutionary force-content distinction with the data-information distinction, the research precisely characterizes the stage-wise ethical issues involved in the phenomenon of speech processing. This results in specifying the framework’s concerns as the ethics of collecting speech data, the ethics of contextualizing speech data or information, and the ethics of releasing the contextualized information or processed speech. Importantly, the paper establishes that the very activity of collecting speech data generates privacy-related ethical concerns, even if the raw data is never transformed into processed information. In the final output stage, the result is a released speech item, spoken item, or text that reaches a moral agent whose subsequent actions are impacted.

The application of this framework successfully clarifies the nature of ethical issues inherent in speech processing, allowing for a robust distinction between immediate and distant ethical concerns, including those arising from Speaker Independent speech technologies. Given the rapid development in speech technology, the resulting novel ethical issues often face moral agents with indeterminate and unfamiliar governing rules; this is exemplified by cases where the entire teleological path of the technology cannot be conceived or made available to the consumer, leading to questions of trust. Furthermore, the use of speech processing in sensitive domains, such as Health Recommender Systems, raises specific ethical issues concerning the autonomy of patients.
