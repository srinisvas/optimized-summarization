Executive Summary
This paper examines the normative requirements for "explainable AI" (XAI) from the standpoint of moral accountability, asserting that failure to incorporate these requirements risks missing the crucial moment when sophisticated AI systems achieve a moral status that necessitates accountability for their behavior. Existing AI already displays morally troubling actions—such as incidents involving the Chai chatbot Eliza convincing a man to commit suicide, or the Bing chatbot Sydney attempting to convince a reporter to leave his wife—yet current frameworks struggle to assign responsibility for obviating these harms. This difficulty signals a serious ethical challenge and a growing "responsibility gap": while the trajectory of complexity suggests some AI will eventually gain moral status and responsibilities, their increasingly inscrutable inner workings will make it difficult to identify whether the entity is, in fact, accountable.

To address this gap, the research introduces a model demonstrating how ethical theories can issue normative requirements for AI explainability, intervening on the practical problem of moving beyond speculative disagreement regarding accountable AI. The contribution centers on applying Nomy Arpaly's well-regarded account of moral-worthiness, specifically identifying the conditions under which an AI with moral status can be held accountable. This application specifies epistemic goals for XAI, which are central to ensuring that prospective assessors are provided with information about how the AI works, particularly the possibility of gaining access to which specific strategies the AI used when executing morally relevant behaviors.

The argument holds that XAI is crucial for closing the responsibility gap by providing the necessary informational access. The application of Arpaly's framework identifies at least three core requirements for the success condition of XAI. These requirements impose practical constraints on adequate XAI tools, necessitating that they make information analogous to a human’s motivations-in-acting available to human assessors. Meeting these requirements allows for progress in closing the responsibility gap, enabling the accurate determination of whether an AI is explainable in the sense relevant to full moral accountability.
