**Executive Summary**

This paper introduces the AI Moral Code, a comprehensive methodological and empirical framework designed for ethical AI governance and system design, structured around the Normative, Regulatory, Behavioral, and Conceptual (NRBC) architecture. The research synthesized a vast corpus of 291 global AI ethics documents assembled between 2006 and 2025, drawing from government strategies, industry guidelines, academic publications, and NGO frameworks. Through recurrence analysis and semantic alignment, the framework identified high-frequency values, establishing justice, transparency, responsibility, nonmaleficence, and inclusivity as the five most persistent cross-sectoral values across domains such as healthcare, education, justice, and autonomous vehicle technologies. By embedding these statistically persistent ethical themes into a layered architecture, the model enables structural consistency while preserving normative diversity, making global coordination feasible without demanding full ethical convergence.

The primary contribution of the AI Moral Code lies in its ability to synthesize philosophical traditions, regulatory systems, and deployment scenarios into a unified ethical structure that is both technically actionable and culturally adaptable. The NRBC architecture functions as a critical middle layer, translating ethical principles into operational policy recommendations aligned with existing oversight mechanisms, addressing regional, philosophical, and institutional variation. This structured approach ensures that values can be interpreted and applied within distinct institutional systems, supporting evidence-based value alignment across sectors—from China's strategic planning aligning deployment with national ethical norms, to sectoral policies like the White House Office of Management and Budget’s emphasis on responsible AI procurement. The framework operationalizes ethics not as a conceptual gesture, but as a requirement for deployment, ensuring that ethical benchmarks, even while regionalized, are grounded in consistent themes.

The framework’s internal coherence and contextual responsiveness were demonstrated through structured simulations reflecting real-world asymmetries and moral entanglements, including one that explored trust, transparency, and inclusivity in adaptive learning platforms. Furthermore, portions of the AI Moral Code have already moved into practical application, having been implemented and tested within the National Centers of Academic Excellence -Cyber (NCAE-C) programs, including a grant-supported initiative at Norwich University in 2024 focused on cybersecurity team formation. The conclusion emphasizes that the next phase of development requires moving beyond simulation to multi-institutional validation, demanding domain-specific replication, stakeholder evaluation, and longitudinal assessment. Future research efforts will focus on expanding real-world pilot deployments, refining traceability mechanisms and transparency metrics, and addressing emergent dilemmas associated with AGI development and AI-human moral co-decision systems.
