Executive Summary
Artificial Intelligence (AI) Ethics is a critical, nascent research field defined as the study of the ethical and responsible development and deployment of AI technology. Given the necessity for a renewed look at the field following recent developments in generative AI and foundational models, this study performs a bibliometric analysis of AI Ethics literature spanning the last 20 years, using keyword search patterns to chart the field’s trajectory. This analysis, which selected a total of 2,517 articles after excluding literature unlisted on SCOPUS, contributes unique insights by mapping the evolution of the field from its historical context, which includes AI being first coined in 1956 and suffering an "AI Winter" after the 1973 Lighthill Report, up to its current exponential growth since reemerging around 2014.

The bibliometric findings confirm a three-phase development structure: the incubation phase, the phase focused on making AI human-like machines, and the current phase centered on making AI human-centric machines. The study notes that by 2020, AI had surpassed humans in tasks previously thought only humans could master, such as handwriting recognition, speech recognition, and language understanding, triggering stiff competition and an all-out war between Big Tech companies. During the human-centric phase, precautionary keywords showed up very frequently in the literature, including "algorithm bias," "AI bias," and "discrimination" in 2020, followed by "opacity," "responsibility gap," and "social justice" in 2021.

Based on these developmental patterns, the research conjectures that the next phase of AI Ethics is likely to focus on making AI more machine-like as AI matches or surpasses humans intellectually, a condition the authors coin as "machine-like human." To get ahead of the rapid pace of AI technology development, AI ethicists may need to proactively research this area, specifically seeking to prohibit unethical development of machine-augmented non-humans. The conclusion emphasizes the critical need to prevent the development of malicious or malevolent Superintelligence, stressing that even if the risks are extremely low, the consequences of such an event could be incredibly serious.
