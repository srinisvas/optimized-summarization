**Executive Summary**
This research investigates a gap in current engineering education where training predominantly focuses on technical ethics, neglecting the holistic development of the whole person, often resulting in a lack of student interest and contextual depth. Recognizing the pivotal role engineers play in society, this study proposes and pilots a virtue-based character education approach, which offers personal motivation and actionable dimensions to ethical reasoning, addressing the challenge that limited previous studies have explored this method in undergraduate engineering courses. The core contribution is the development of structured teaching modules that utilize stories from the traditional culture of different countries to help students identify inherent virtues and connect them directly to engineering ethics. These modules integrate ethical training into students' coursework via "virtue-of-the-week" stories and team-based activities, ensuring that ethics becomes an essential and related component of problem-solving rather than an isolated subject.

The pilot study, conducted within a capstone design course, suggests that this novel approach successfully helps students make the important connection between being a virtuous person and being an ethical engineer. Preliminary findings indicate high student engagement and interest, demonstrating that connecting engineering ethics to virtues—concepts students have learned since childhood—makes the subject more relatable and provides richer context through stories from diverse cultures. For example, modules aimed to reinforce virtues such as diligence, perseverance, empathy, and kindness, resulting in students becoming more considerate and hardworking team members in their projects. This integrated model provides students with a richer context for ethical decision-making, grounding their professional choices more closely in their personal character, thereby addressing the lack of interest found in traditional approaches focused solely on codes of ethics and case studies.

This investigation advances knowledge in the field by showing how traditional cultural stories can inform students’ ethical understanding and decision-making, with the developed teaching modules serving as useful resources for other engineering faculty. While the preliminary results are encouraging, the pilot study’s small sample size, comprising only 50 Chemical Engineering (CHE) and 20 Environmental Engineering (ENVE) students, is noted as a limitation. Future work will involve a thorough analysis of the gathered data to assess the modules' effectiveness, the development of more rigorous assessments to quantify improvements in ethical understanding, and the creation of additional modules focused specifically on teaching ethical decision-making, such as incorporating the consideration of virtues into ethics case studies.
