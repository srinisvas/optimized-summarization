**Executive Summary**
The emergence of the #StopHateForProfit campaign in June 2020, which saw numerous companies withdraw their advertising from major social media platforms, particularly Facebook, due to the platforms' failures to address the proliferation of hateful content, necessitates an academic reflection on an understudied topic: the ethics of boycotting by corporations. While applied ethics has generated significant recent discussion on value-driven consumerism, this focus has been almost exclusively confined to the consumption choices of individuals. The events of 2020 extended the debate over the ethical obligations of technology corporations to include the duties of those who support them financially, establishing a critical test case for various positions on corporate social responsibility (CSR).

The paper begins by arguing that Facebook was involved in an injustice significant enough, at least through June 2020, to raise serious questions regarding the complicity of its advertisers. While ethical views regarding individuals suggest that boycotts are a permissible form of political expression and may even be morally required under certain conditions, the authors contend that boycotting by companies introduces additional complex considerations. The central finding suggests that boycotting by corporations may sometimes be morally required, generating an obligation that directly challenges the plausibility of certain minimalist conceptions of CSR, while simultaneously revealing the limits of "corporate citizenship" conceptions that promote forceful activism for social change.

To address this complexity, the research proposes preliminary principles intended to guide the ethics of boycotting by corporations. These principles indicate how corporate boycotts relate to different CSR paradigms and contribute fundamentally to ongoing efforts aimed at improving the accountability of technology corporations. Crucially, the analysis notes a significant political risk: boycotting by corporations may also threaten democratic forms of political contestation by inhibiting public deliberation and equal participation in the resolution of political controversies. This observation underscores how value-driven consumerism enacted by business corporations complicates these issues and warrants further research.
