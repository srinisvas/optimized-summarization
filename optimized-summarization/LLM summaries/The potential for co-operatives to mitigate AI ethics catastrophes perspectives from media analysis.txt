Executive Summary

This paper investigates whether adopting a co-operative organizational model could mitigate the systemic ethical failures observed in the AI industry, arguing that existing incremental multi-stakeholder ethics efforts may fail to address the fundamental issues rooted in traditional corporate power structures. Through an inductive media analysis focusing on high-profile AI-related catastrophes between 2018 and 2021, the research establishes that the vast majority (71%) of these recent ethics scandals were not due to a lack of knowledge or technical tools, but were instead attributed to power dynamics that hinder the ability of internal stakeholders to take corrective action. This systemic problem persists because ethical decision-making, particularly in the competitive AI field, is frequently treated as an afterthought or a public relations exercise, contrasting sharply with co-operatives where the business owners are the members—potentially workers, users, or other stakeholders—rather than external shareholders who often have little connection to the company’s day-to-day operations.

The study posits the co-operative governance structure, core to which is democratic governance and distributive executive power, as a potential mitigating solution, capable of fostering a more democratic power structure for the design of AI systems. The primary advantage lies in the legal implications of this structure, as democratic governance is ratified within the company's articles of incorporation, offering members "institutional whistleblower protection" that is superior to purely principle-based approaches like codes of ethics. Specifically, if a co-operative appears to violate Principle 3, Democratic Member Control, a member is legally empowered to escalate the issue through resolution, mediation, or litigation. Furthermore, the analysis suggests that co-op models can directly address four and partially help address five of the twelve identified categories of issues related to AI ethics scandals, noting that the cooperative structure incorporates members who are better equipped to critically engage with the unique circumstances of novel ethical issues.

Despite its benefits, the co-op model does not mandate specific ethics requirements; ethical behavior is rather a potential side effect resulting from distributing power and risk. The model is not infallible, as demonstrated by internal corruption scandals, and does not hold an advantage in addressing all issues, particularly those concerning incitement of government change or mitigating the consequences of failure (Theme 3), which are no different than in a traditional company. Additionally, co-operatives remain subject to the same larger systemic issues present in society, such as those related to implicit racism or toxic workplace culture. Nevertheless, the research proposes a multi-stakeholder co-operative, including workers, users, and potentially data producers, as the ideal structure to make meaningful and actionable progress by distributing power to those who possess a direct stake in AI development and those who directly suffer the risks of AI failure.
