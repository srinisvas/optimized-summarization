**Executive Summary**

This paper addresses the persistent challenge of demonstrating and measuring privacy compliance in existing cloud architectures, a significant hurdle given the substantial regulatory punishments associated with data protection violations and the resulting organizational reluctance to migrate sensitive data. While numerous privacy design strategies have been proposed, evaluating the appropriate implementation of these high-level tactics within operational systems remains difficult. To mitigate this gap, the authors propose a novel approach based on "privacy smells," which are specific indicators of a system's potential non-compliance with established privacy design strategies. The research identifies concrete metrics derived from these strategies and defines smells based on these metrics, arguing that certain privacy aspects in cloud systems can be mapped to useful quantitative indicators of underlying problems, thereby enabling cloud users and auditors to detect deep-rooted privacy issues.

The methodology identifies indicators at two critical levels of a cloud system: the data flow level and the access control level. Focusing on the access control mechanism common in cloud computing, specifically Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), metrics are proposed to identify potential issues such as overlapping permissions among user groups, measured by quantifying the proportion of shared (permissions, scope)-tuples. Additionally, the concept of "Data broadcasts" is introduced as a smell to identify groups of users and resources that have accessed a specific data item in the past. The technical viability of measuring these indicators is demonstrated using a system built in Microsoft Azure, with differences relative to other major cloud providers like Amazon Web Services and Google Cloud Platform also discussed.

The significance of this approach lies in its ability to provide tangible, measurable indicators for a concept—privacy—that is often abstract. Although the paper does not propose precise fixed thresholds for smell detection, cloud users can apply custom expected thresholds tailored to their system’s requirements to identify potential privacy problems. Furthermore, these metrics can be utilized to monitor respective privacy aspects over time to detect significant changes, and auditors can leverage these indicators of potential issues to select a relevant sample of the system for focused inspection. The work concludes that measuring indicators of potential problems through metrics and privacy smells based on common design strategies is a valuable approach for finding and correcting privacy problems related to non-compliance.
