**Executive Summary**

This paper analyzes the crucial, yet often overlooked, bidirectional interaction between ethics and technology, arguing that ethics exerts a powerful influence through professional channels, consumer behavior, and legal mechanisms, which collectively shape the direction, scope, and implementation of science and engineering projects, exemplified by the establishment of codes of professional ethics. Conversely, technology has profoundly affected ethical theory; advancements in science and technology are posited as underlying reactions for abstract ideas, including normative ethical theories, and have heavily influenced modern relativistic ethics. By providing new possibilities for human life and simultaneously creating novel moral quandaries, technological development has essentially generated the entire domain of "applied ethics."

The central analysis focuses on stressing the critical importance of ethics, particularly within the field of software engineering, where specific areas of concern arise, such as the unauthorized use of computer resources to steal, damnify people, or commit fraud. Modern computer technology has advanced to a phase where virtually any development must be viewed as having significant, non-trivial consequences, especially given that almost every profession now relies on specialized applications to carry out daily functions. This dependency has unfortunately coincided with the rise of modern technology crooks who exploit vulnerabilities to steal credit card information and commit fraud, often facilitated by limited regulation; consequently, just focusing on creating code that can compile and complete a job is insufficient for today's entry-level programmer.

The discussion emphasizes the complexity of contemporary ethical decision-making in technology, noting that situations may arise where an action appears ethical yet results in negative repercussions. A fundamental ethical test involves balancing security outcomes against personal liberty, such as developing a program that halts cybercrime but requires the complete surrender of personal privacy for all internet users—an outcome where social efficacy alone would not constitute a good measurement. These marginal ethical subjects must be addressed during initial interactive meetings with clients, affirming the paper’s goal to analyze this different view of the ethics of science and technology without requiring differentiation between basic terms like ethics, morals, and values in the context of computer ethics discussion.
